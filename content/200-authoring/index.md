## Write it for the closer

Marcus is off Thursday. Somebody else is grading the crate. What do you leave taped to the counter?

Not a policy document. Not a philosophy of record grading. You leave the four things that person will get wrong, the format you want it written up in, and the one edge case that comes up often enough to matter.

That's a skill. Skills that read like corporate documentation perform badly, because documentation is written to be complete and a skill needs to be written to be *followed*.

<div class="pair">
<div class="no">
<p class="eyebrow">Documentation brain</p>
<p>"Condition grading is an important part of the resale process and helps ensure customer satisfaction. Our shop follows industry-standard practices to provide accurate and consistent assessments across all inventory categories."</p>
</div>
<div class="yes">
<p class="eyebrow">Shift-note brain</p>
<p>"Grade the disc and the sleeve separately, never as one number. A scratch you can catch a fingernail on caps the disc at VG. If you can't tell, ask. Don't guess."</p>
</div>
</div>

The first one is 44 words that change nothing. The second is 38 words that change the output.

## The four questions to answer before writing

<div class="grid grid-2">
<div class="card">
<h4>1. What would somebody actually type?</h4>
<p>Write those words down. They go in the description, near-verbatim. This is the single highest leverage thing you can do.</p>
</div>
<div class="card">
<h4>2. What does the output need to look like?</h4>
<p>If you can show the shape, show the shape. A format block beats three paragraphs about tone.</p>
</div>
<div class="card">
<h4>3. What does a new hire get wrong?</h4>
<p>Those are your rules. Not every rule, just the ones somebody breaks.</p>
</div>
<div class="card">
<h4>4. When should this <em>not</em> run?</h4>
<p>The most skipped question and the source of most "why did it do that" moments.</p>
</div>
</div>
